Characteristics of tongue coating microbiota in diabetic and non-diabetic kidney patients receiving hemodialysis

Abstract
Background
Tongue-coating microbiota, especially known as the tongue microbiome, holds significant value as both a prospective clinical diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target, which plays a crucial role in the oral microecological health. However, there is limited understanding of the composition and function of tongue coating microbiota in chronic kidney disease patients undergoing hemodialysis
Methods
Thirty-one non-diabetic hemodialysis patients (nonDM_HD), 29 diabetic hemodialysis patients (DM_HD) and 33 healthy controls (HC) were enrolled. Swabs from tongue coating were collected. The 16S rDNA (V3-V4 region) was sequenced to scrutinize the tongue-coating bacterial microbiome difference. Results
Both nonDM_HD and DM_HD showed distinct bacterial communities of oral microbiota compared to HC
The abundance of Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Ruminococcaceae G1, Ligilactobacillus and Abiotrophia showed a significant increase (p < 0. 05) in DM_HD and nonDM_HD compared to HC, while Haemophilus, Lachnoanaerobaculum, Peptostreptococcaceae G1, Peptostreptococcus showed a significant decrease (p < 0. 05) respectively. Veillonella, Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus etc. may serve as potential biomarkers for DM_HD
While Streptococcus, Ruminococcaceae G1, Actinobacillus, Abiotrophia can be considered alternative biomarkers for nonDM_HD. Moreover, the enriched Haemophilus, Actinomyces, Lachnoanaerobaculum were prominent features of the tongue coating microbiota in HC, which could be used as the potential therapeutic targets of chronic kidney disease. Network analysis revealed a less complex interaction relationship among the tongue coating bacterial microbiota of nonDM_HD and DM_HD
Furthermore, correlations were identified between the microbiome composition and clinical parameters of the individuals. Conclusion
In conclusion, deciphering the tongue coating microbiota of kidney patients undergoing hemodialysis will helpful in assessing the role of oral microbiota in pathobiology and development of kidney disease, which is expected to become a potential biomarkers and adjuvant therapeutic target
With persistently deteriorating pathogenic conditions, chronic kidney disease can progress to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). Hemodialysis, a common renal replacement therapy, can improve the health of patients with ESKD and promote their long-term survival [2]. Additionally, there is a close relationship between diabetes and chronic kidney disease, with nearly 40% of diabetes patients developing diabetic kidney disease [3]
The significance of the gut microbiome in human diseases has been extensively documented [4, 5], highlighting its substantial influence on health. The relationship between the gut microbiome and chronic kidney disease has been thoroughly examined [6, 7]. Oral health is closely related to the human health, the study of oral-gut axis has more and more favored by researchers in recent years [8–10], as saliva containing the oral microbiome is constantly swallowed and released into the digestive tract
Many studies have suggested that the oral microbiome also affects the host’s health [11–14]. The occurrence of some diseases will lead to changes in oral microbiota, and conversely, the imbalance of oral microbiota will also aggravate or affect the occurrence and development of some diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, cancer and gastrointestinal disorders [15–19]. Oral microbiota will be a potential target for the control of oral-gut dysbiosis and other chronic systemic inflammation [20]
Furthermore, several previous studies have discussed the association between the oral microbiome and patients with chronic kidney disease [1, 21–23]. Duan et al. discovered changes in the salivary microbiome, related to oral health state, in patients with end-stage renal disease [21]. Additionally, periodontal pathogens are more prevalent in patients undergoing hemodialysis [21]. Guo et al
found that patients with chronic kidney disease had increased oral microbial diversity, and certain genera showed changes in abundance [22]. Recently, Xiang et al. have provided evidence supporting the association between salivary microbiota and renal transplant recipients. Their study revealed that specific species of salivary microbiota could potentially serve as biomarkers for postoperative recovery [24]. The potential impact of the salivary microbiome on diabetes has been discussed [25, 26]
For example, Liu et al. investigated the influence of diabetes on the salivary microbiome in chronic kidney disease and reported a distinct bacterial community in the saliva of patients compared to the control group. However, no differences were found between chronic kidney disease patients with and without diabetes [1]. Tongue coating microbiota plays a significant role in the composition of oral microbiota, closely associated with saliva microbiota [27, 28]
Extensive discussions have been conducted on the impact of the oral microbiome on human health and disease, attracting considerable attention [29]. Some research has unveiled the association of oral microbiome with various diseases, such as colorectal cancer [30], gastritis [31], gastric cancer [28], halitosis [32], pancreatic cancer [27], oral squamous cell carcinoma(OSCC) [33–35], and osteoradionecrosis [36]
These findings indicated that the tongue coating microbiome could serve as a promising non-invasive biomarker for diagnosing certain diseases. However, there have been limited studies investigating the relationship between the tongue coating microbiome and chronic kidney disease in patients with diabetes. Thus, the present study aims to investigate the difference in tongue coating microbiome among DM_HD, nonDM_HD and HC
The findings enhance our understanding of how diabetes and kidney disease affect the oral microbiome of hemodialysis patients, and shed light on the potential interaction between variations in tongue coating microbiome and healthy individuals. Materials and methods
Ethics and study subjects
The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University granted approval for this study (Approval Number 2022-KLS-228–01)
Prior to their participation, all participants provided written informed consent, and the study was carried out in compliance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. The subjects in this study were patients who visited the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese medical university for hemodialysis. According to the KDIGO criteria, individuals with a glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 ml/(min·1
73m^2), and the time of receiving hemodialysis is more than 3 months were considered inclusion CKD patients. Then following ADA standards, CKD patients with random blood glucose levels ≥ 11. 1mmol/L or HbA1 ≥ 6. 5% were categorized into the diabetic hemodialysis patients (DM_HD) group, while those with lower levels were classified into the non-diabetic hemodialysis patients (non DM_HD) group
In addition, the other inclusion criteria for the case were shown as follows: diabetic or non-diabetic patients with hemodialysis, no less than 20 teeth, including at least one molar and one premolar in each quadrant of the dentition, no removable partial denture, no extensive pathological changes in the oral mucosa, no history of antibiotics use for more than 3 months prior to sampling, no history of smoking, no respiratory, hepatic, renal or gastrointestinal disorders, no severe mental illness
The inclusion criteria for healthy control (HC) group were as follows: annual physical examination with no abnormalities delected, an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) greater than 90 ml/ (min·1
73m^2), normal urine routine and renal imaging examination, no chronic diseases history damaging kidney (diabetes, hyperuricemia, and rheumatic immune diseases), no history of kidney transplantation, no active inflammation, no use of antibiotics or immunosuppressants within the previous three months, and no obvious pathogens infection
All included individuals from the healthy population had healthy oral cavities, which characterized by clean teeth, absence of dental caries, no pain, normal gum color, and no signs of bleeding. In order to explore the impact of diabetes on the tongue coating microbiome among patients undergoing hemodialysis, we have categorized the participants into three distinct groups: DM_HD, nonDM_HD, and HC
Sample collection and DNA isolation
After oral cavity examination, participants rinsed their mouths with pure water and breathed for 30 s with their mouths closed, a collection of tongue coating samples was then performed with a sterile swab (MS-OF3602, PS bar + flocking head, Zhejiang Cixi Huabao Medical Supplies Co., LTD, China) according to a previous report [37]. A total of 29 DM_HD, 31 nonDM_HD and 33 HC samples were collected
The sample was collected in sterile plastic tubes and stored at −80℃ for further analysis. Professional training of sampling for dentists was performed to ensure internal consistency. 16S rDNA amplification and sequencing
Total DNA was extracted from tongue coating samples using the E. Z. N. A. ®Stool DNA Kit (D4015, Omega, Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions
The reagent which was designed to uncover DNA from trace amounts of the sample has been shown to be effective for the preparation of DNA of most bacteria. Nuclear-free water was used for blank. The total DNA was eluted in 50 μL of Elution buffer and stored at −80 °C until measurement in the PCR by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd, Hang Zhou, Zhejiang Province, China. The V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rDNA gene were PCR amplified using barcoded 341F and 805R [38]
PCR amplification was performed in a total volume of 25 μL reaction mixture containing 25 ng template DNA, 12. 5 μL PCR Premix, 2. 5 μL each primer, and PCR-grade water to adjust the volume. The PCR conditions to amplify the prokaryotic 16S fragments consisted of an initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 s; 32cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 54 oC for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; and then final extension at 72 °C for 10 min
The PCR products were confirmed with 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Throughout the DNA extraction process, ultrapure water, instead of a sample solution, was used to exclude the possibility of false-positive PCR results as a negative control. The PCR products were purified by AMPure XT beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA) and quantified by Qubit (Invitrogen, USA)
The amplicon pools were prepared for sequencing and the size and quantity of the amplicon library were assessed on Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA) and with the Library Quantification Kit for Illumina (Kapa Biosciences, Woburn, MA, USA), respectively. The libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq PE250 platform. Bioinformatics and statistical analysis
The samples were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, provided by LC-Bio
Paired-end reads were assigned to samples based on their unique barcode and truncated by cutting off the barcode and primer sequence. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH [39]. Quality filtering on the raw reads was performed under specific filtering conditions to obtain the high-quality clean tags according to the fqtrim (v0. 94). Chimeric sequences were filtered using Vsearch software (v2. 3. 4) [40]. After dereplication using DADA2, we obtained the feature table and feature sequence
Alpha diversity and beta diversity were calculated by normalizing to the same sequences randomly. Then according to HOME classifier, feature abundance was normalized using the relative abundance of each sample. chao1, pielou_e, Shannon and phylogenetic diversity index were used to evaluate the richness, evenness, and diversity of the tongue coating microbiome in the groups, and all these indexes were calculated with QIIME2
Principal-coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed based on the bray_curtis_distance using vegan and ade4 package of R. The dissimilarity between groups was evaluated using the analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) based on bray_curtis_distance using vegan package of R. Blast was used for sequence alignment, and the feature sequences were annotated with the HOME database for each representative sequence [41]
The one-against-all (less strict) LDA effect size (LEfSe) was calculated and plotted using OmicStudio tools. In addition, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve used to illustrate the diagnostic ability of bacterial genus was performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www. omicstudio. cn/tool/58. A bacterial interaction network (using Sparcc) was performed using the OmicStudio tools with a Sparcc correlation coefficient (|correlation r)|> 0. 1, and p < 0
05), and the network map was made using Gephi 0. 10. Prediction of the Microbial functional was undertaken using the PICRUSt2 by searching data on the 16S rDNA gene sequencing in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (www. genome. jp/). Advanced cor link of clinical indicators and microbiome was performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www. omicstudio. cn/tool. Other diagrams were implemented using the R package (v3. 5. 2)
Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test was used for a nonparametric comparison between the two groups (p < 0. 05). T test was used for parametric comparison between the two groups (p < 0. 05). Kruskal–Wallis. Test was used for multiple group comparisons (p < 0. 05). Results

After normalizing and rarifying the sequences, we obtained 12,619, 12,948, and 12,320 ASVs in the DM_HD, nonDM_HD, and HC groups, respectively. The DM_HD and HC shared 1,910 ASVs, nonDM_HD and HC shared 1,813 ASVs, and DM_HD shared 2,112 ASVs with nonDM_HD individually. Additionally, 1,494 ASVs were shared by all three groups (Supplementary Fig. 1). We observed similarities and differences in the composition of the tongue coating microbiota among the three groups
The Good’s coverage index in each sample had a value of 100% measured at the ASV level (Supplementary Fig. 2A). The rarefaction curves demonstrated that the microbial richness of the oral samples approached saturation at the utilized sequencing depth (Supplementary Fig. 2B), which was deemed adequate for identifying the majority of bacterial community members within each microbiota. Alpha and beta diversity analysis of the tongue coating microbiome
According to the chao_1 index (Fig
1A), there was no significant difference in richness among the groups. The pielou_e index (Fig. 1B) also showed no significant difference in evenness. Similarly, the Shannon index (Fig. 1C) showed that there was no significant difference in diversity between groups. However, there was a significant difference in the phylogenetic diversity index between nonDM_HD and HC (p < 0. 05) (Fig. 1D)
The rank_abundance curve proved that no significant difference in evenness and diversity was observed (Supplementary Fig. 2C). In addition, the principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and Anosim analysis (Fig. 1E) were performed to further compare the microbial community composition
Although no significant separation in PCoA diagram was observed among groups, the anosim analysis revealed a significant difference, which indicated that nonDM_HD and DM_HD both displayed significantly different bacterial communities compared to HC, but no differences were observed between nonDM_HD and DM_HD. With the deepening of the association study between the human microbiome and diseases, the researchers are paying more attention on role of oral microbiome in human health gradually
There is growing evidences suggesting that the oral microbiome can serve as a potential clinical diagnostic marker and therapeutic target for evaluating oral and systemic diseases [20, 29, 42]. Several studies have elaborated on the relationship between oral microbiome dysbiosis and various ailments, such as type 2 diabetes and inflammatory bowel diseases [11, 33, 34, 43, 44]
Furthermore, considering the association between oral microbiota and gut microbiota, the positive modulation of oral microbiota could represent a new possible adjuvant therapy in the clinical management of kidney disease [42]. Therefore, it may be worthwhile to further explore targeted therapies for regulating oral microbiota and gut microbiota dysbiosis in order to alleviate oral and gut diseases, so as potential to assist the clinical management of chronic kidney disease
In this study, we investigated the characteristics of the tongue coating microbiome in hemodialysis patients, both with diabetes and without diabetes. We analyzed 93 tongue coating samples and found no significant differences in the richness, evenness, and diversity (except for phylogenetic diversity) of the microbiome between three groups, which differs somewhat from previous studies. Both Guo et al. and Duan et al. reported higher diversity in patients’ oral microbiomes, with Duan et al
also finding a decrease in abundance, but Liu et al. did not observe any changes [1, 21, 22]. Therefore, no consistent conclusion could be drawn regarding the alteration in richness and diversity. Both nonDM_HD and DM_HD patients demonstrated a disparate bacterial community and bacterial diversity compared to those in health control group, which is similar to previous studies [1, 21, 22]
However, we did not observe any differences in the bacterial community between DM_HD and nonDM_HD, which is also consistent with former research [1]. Regarding bacterial taxa, several discrepancies and similarities, including proportion and variation tendency, were found in our results compared to other studies on chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients. We found that the relative abundance of Bacillota ranked first among all groups, which was the same as the study by Liu et al. and Duan et al
, but it ranked third in the study by Guo et al. [1, 21, 22]. The abundance of Bacillota was significantly increased in CKD patients, which was also found in our results [22]. Furthermore, the Streptococcus, Neisseria and Prevotella were dominant in our research, which coincides with other studies [1, 21, 22]. Some research found the abundance of Prevotella significantly decreased in oral microbiota of CKD [1, 21, 22], but we did not observe it
Actinomyces and Haemophilus decreased significantly in nonDM_HD, which is consistent with the results of Liu et al. and Guo et al. respectively [1, 22]. In summary, the primary causes for the observed discrepancies are that the subjects of this study were chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients undergoing hemodialysis, and moreover, the samples of tongue coating might exhibit minor variations compared to saliva samples
Obviously, our results indicated Streptococcus accounts for a significant proportion of the relative abundance and increased tremendously in nonDM_HD and DM_HD, which is similar to some previous research [1, 22]. Streptococcus has been proven to be associated with various ailments. Casarin et al. observed a high abundance of Streptococcus in the oral cavity of type 2 diabetic patients [45]. Hu et al
determined that the abundance of Streptococcus in saliva decreased in chronic kidney disease patients, and they also noticed negative correlation between eGFR and Streptococcus, suggesting a negative impact on kidney function [46]. Several studies have explored the correlation between Streptococcus and cancer. For example, Streptococcus anginosus may serve as a noninvasive biomarker for oropharyngeal cancer [13], while S. mitis increased in with OSCC [47]. Besides, Goel et al
found a higher abundance of S. salivarius in the saliva of patients with Orofacial Granulomatosis (OFG) or Crohn’s Disease (CD) [46]. Other researchers have also discussed the probiotic functions of S. salivarius, showing its strong inhibition against S. pyogenes, a principal pathogen in the human oral cavity [48]. These means several Streptococcus species that may hold promise as potencial biomarkers in both DM_HD and nonDM_HD
It seems that a deeper identification and analysis the relationship of some Streptococcus taxa and related diseases are highly required, due to the diverse functions of Streptococcus in oral and systemic diseases. In addition, Lactobacillus and Ligilactobacillus were both enriched in DM_HD and nonDM_HD. At the same time, Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, and Limosilactobacillus are all considered biomarkers of DM_HD
Lactobacillus reuteri (Limolactobacillus reuteri) is widely recognized for its probiotic benefits and its ability to improve human health [49]. Due to the antimicrobial activity, Lactobacillus reuteri can inhibit the colonization of pathogens, and reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and simultaneously promote the development and function of regulatory T cells. Therefore, inflammatory diseases can be alleviated by increase the colonization of Lactobacillus reuteri [50]
Limosilactobacillus reuteri has also been proven to improve the oral cavity`s health [51]. In some research on kidney disease, Tsai et al. found that Lactobacillus rhamnosus GKLC1 could be used as an agent to ameliorate the development of chronic kidney disease in the mice by reducing inflammation, inhibiting apoptosis, and ameliorating fibrosis [52]. Ahmad Gholami et al
discovered that Limosilactobacillus fermentum and its supernatant postbiotic could provide protection against oxidative stress in mice with chronic kidney disease [53]. In addition, a study found that the levels of Ligilactobacillus increased in CKD rats after dietary treatment [54]. Meanwhile, there are several studies indicating that Lactobacillus spp
which is involved in the process of immunomodulation, are drastically reduced in diabetic kidney disease [55]; while the level of Lactobacillus is reduced in type 1 diabetic mellitus [56, 57]. In summary, these results suggests the above three genera have the potential to serve as targets for improving the diagnosis, intervention, and treatment of diabetes and kidney disease. According to the LEfSe analysis. Haemophilus and Actinomyces were more abundant in HC than two patient groups
However, Haemophilus, as a potential pathogen, which has been associated with various severe diseases [58–60]. Actinomyces is closely related to infection [61], extraradicular lesions [62], endocarditis [63], pyogenic liver abscess [64, 65] and so on. Whether these genera abundance interventions contribute to the treatment of CKD deserves more further studies
By comparing the prediction function of the tongue coating microbiome, we found DM_HD groups presented significantly lower levels of cellular processes and signaling, biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites, metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides and a higher level of excretory system, which was quite different from other research [1, 21]. Furthermore, some studies found a significant difference in the abundance of metabolism pathway, whereas our results only limitedly reflect this point
In that manner, detailed metabolome analysis is required to assess associations between the oral microbiome and its metabolism. By employed microbial correlation network analysis, we found the reduced complexity of the tongue coating microbiota in nonDM_HD and DM_HD, which has rarely been reported before. A decreasing number of nodes and edges, lower average clustering coefficients, graph density and an exceptionally low number of total triangles were observed
These results suggest that the physiological alterations experienced by patients receiving hemodialysis disrupt the natural balance of oral microbiota and the interaction patterns within the community. This disruption may lead to the considerable proliferation of certain pathogens and probiotics, leading to less complex and stable network
Furthermore, in oral microbiota of patients, pathogenic microorganisms are likely to have correlations with each other and promote some oral diseases, while probiotics may exert an antipathogenic role in maintaining a healthy and stable oral microenvironment. Our findings were consistent with some earlier researches demonstrating that the complexity and modification in correlation pattern likely signified the microbiota dysbiosis and possible disease emergence [66–68]
Furthermore, we analyze the correlations between the oral microbiome and clinical indicators in DM_HD and nonDM_HD. Actinomyces and Abiotrophia are common pathogenic bacteria, showing a positive correlation with white blood cells, suggesting a potential risk of microbiota dysbiosis [59, 69]. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is a crucial marker for assessing kidney function
Our study revealed that Capnocytophaga granulosa, Capnocytophaga sp HMT 332, Leptotrichia buccalis, Leptotrichia sp HMT 463, and Prevotella nanceiensis are positively correlated with eGFR in patients, suggesting these taxa have potential roles in improving kidney disease and the possibility of being used as therapeutic targets or indicators. While the current study provides valuable insights, it does have some limitations
Firstly, since kidney disease is more prevalent among older individuals and our participants were primarily local residents, this study is limited in terms of both sample size and geographical diversity. Secondly, due to constraints in the study design and implementation, we were unable to obtain comprehensive clinical and microbiome data from CKD patients prior to hemodialysis. This limitation prevents us from fully understanding the impact of hemodialysis on the tongue coating microbiome
Conclusions
In conclusion, the tongue coating microbiota of hemodialysis patients, both with and without diabetes, exhibits distinct characteristics compare to health control, and there are also differences between these two CKD patients. The enriched Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Streptococcus sp. EO2001-01 and Lactobacillus sp. L-YJ may serve as potential biomarkers for DM_HD
Additionally, Streptococcus, Actinobacillus, Abiotrophia, Streptococcus mitis and uncultured Abiotrophia sp. could be considered as alternative biomarkers for nonDM_HD. Furthermore, the enriched Haemophilus, Actinomyces, Eubacterium nodatum and Peptostreptococcus were prominent features of the tongue coating microbiota in HC, which can be used as the potential target of oral microecological intervention in patients with CKD
